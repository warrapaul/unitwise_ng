 tenant account dashboard review - what is your opinion on this
The main problem is not a lack of information. It is how the information is prioritized, how much is displayed at once, and whether the figures and actions are clear and trustworthy.

For a tenant logging in, the first questions should be:
1. How much do I owe, and when is it due?
2. Has my payment been received and confirmed?
3. How do I pay my rent?
4. Where do I report a problem or contact my landlord?
5. Where can I find my tenancy agreement, receipts, and other documents?
Your dashboard answers most of these questions, but the hierarchy and a few inconsistencies make the experience harder to use than it needs to be.
1. What is already working well

Useful financial information

The amount due, rent breakdown, payment history, and outstanding balance are visible without navigating through multiple pages.




Good billing transparency

Separating rent, garbage, water, internet, and waived charges helps tenants understand what makes up their bill.


Practical tenant support

Showing the caretaker's contact details and payment instructions is especially useful in a Kenyan rental context, where tenants may pay using M-Pesa, bank transfers, or cash.

A useful tenancy overview

Room details, move-in date, deposit, house rules, and monthly charges give tenants a single place to reference their tenancy information.

The highest-priority problems
These are the changes I would address first, in order of importance.

1. Resolve financial inconsistencies
Critical
The dashboard shows the rent due date as 9 October 2026, while the payment instructions say rent is due on the 5th of every month. Those statements should never conflict.

It also shows KES 7,801 billed, KES 6,000 paid, and KES 1,801 outstanding, while the payment history records KES 6,000 on 9 October. The arithmetic is correct, but the tenant needs to know whether the recorded payment has been applied to the current bill and whether any additional charges are outstanding.

Recommendation: Establish one authoritative billing summary, with consistent due dates, payment allocation, billing periods, and balances across all dashboard sections.


2. Reduce the amount of information shown at once

The page has multiple large cards, tables, and detailed tenancy records. The first viewport already contains several competing sections, while other important details require substantial scrolling.

Recommendation: Prioritize the current bill, due date, payment status, and next action. Keep historical changes, detailed charge explanations, deposit receipts, and room history further down or on dedicated pages.


3. Make payment status unambiguous

The page shows a confirmed rent payment and then says no payments have been reported. These may represent different workflows, but the distinction is not obvious.

Recommendation: Separate landlord-recorded payments from tenant-submitted payment reports, and label each status clearly: Confirmed, Awaiting confirmation, Rejected, or Not reported.


4. Give the tenant a clearer primary action

“Report a payment” is useful, but the dashboard does not make it obvious what the tenant should do next when there is an overdue balance.

Recommendation: Make the primary action contextual: View payment instructions or Pay now when payment is due, Submit payment details when payment has been made but is unreported, and View receipt when payment is confirmed. Only show actions the application actually supports.


5. Improve trust and data quality

Some visible details, including bld1, “Default,” “mtttty,” and the generic caretaker name, look like test data or internal identifiers. The displayed M-Pesa reference also needs to be presented consistently with the transaction record.

4. What is missing from a production-ready tenant dashboard?
The current dashboard is strong on financial information, but it is less complete when it comes to managing the tenant's relationship with the property and the landlord.

I would prioritize the following features.
Tenancy documents

Give tenants access to their signed tenancy agreement, renewal documents, inventory or move-in inspection report, deposit receipt, rent receipts, and downloadable statements. Show document status and expiry dates where relevant.

Upcoming obligations

Show the next rent due date, upcoming scheduled inspections or maintenance visits, lease renewal dates, and relevant notice deadlines. Only show events that actually exist.


Communication history

Tenants should be able to find previous messages and announcements, not just start a chat. Important notices from the landlord should remain accessible after the notification disappears.


Relevant notifications

Surface unpaid bills, payment confirmations, maintenance updates, expiring agreements, and important property announcements. Each notification should lead to the relevant record or action.


Tenant account and household details

Provide a clear path to update contact details, review authorized occupants where applicable, and manage account security. Sensitive tenancy information should only be visible to authorized users.



Not all of these need to be visible on the dashboard at all times. The dashboard should summarize them and link to the relevant dedicated pages.
5. Visual design and layout improvements
The existing design uses a consistent green and neutral palette, rounded cards, and a familiar sidebar layout. I would retain that foundation rather than redesigning the whole application.
Reduce oversized cards

The large empty areas in “Who to call” and “Your home” make the dashboard feel stretched. Let cards size to their content where appropriate, and align related sections to a common grid.




Improve typography and spacing

Use a consistent type scale for page titles, card headings, body text, secondary labels, and table values. The current typography is generally readable, but the page contains many competing bold labels.

Make financial tables easier to scan

Right-align monetary values, consistently format all KES amounts, use restrained row separators, and visually distinguish totals from ordinary charges. Keep secondary explanations smaller but readable.

Improve responsive behavior

The screenshots show a wide desktop viewport. Verify that the two-column layout collapses into a single column on tablets and phones, the sidebar becomes a drawer, tables remain usable, and payment actions stay accessible.



Additional visual refinements
- Use one primary button style, one secondary style, and a consistent icon-button treatment.
- Keep status colors consistent: green for confirmed or active, amber for pending, red for overdue or rejected, and neutral for informational states.
- Never communicate a status through color alone.
- Use consistent date formats, for example 9 Oct 2026, and consistent currency formatting, such as KES 6,000.
- Avoid showing internal identifiers unless tenants need them.
- Ensure keyboard navigation, visible focus states, meaningful button labels, adequate contrast, and screen-reader-friendly tables.
- Replace generic or placeholder data before launch.

Not in every case.

I would use the following rules:

Callback result

UnitWise action

Valid transaction, unique reference, room identified, no conflicting claim

Match automatically and apply the configured confirmation policy.

Valid transaction and room identified, but no tenant claim exists

Record the transaction as received and unmatched to a claim; let the landlord allocate it or apply an approved automatic-allocation rule.

Amount differs from the claim

Flag the discrepancy for review.

Reference identifies no room

Keep it in the unmatched queue.

Transaction ID already processed

Treat as a duplicate callback; do not create another payment.

Payment reference is correct but bill is already paid

Flag for review or credit allocation; never silently double-allocate.

There is an important distinction here: the landlord may choose to enable automatic confirmation for clean, unambiguous payments. But matching a reference alone is not sufficient to establish that a payment settles the intended amount or bill.

Also, a bank transaction pasted into UnitWise is not equivalent to an authenticated bank callback. For bank payments, automatic confirmation should depend on the available verification mechanism.

6. How to handle agencies with multiple buildings
There are two viable approaches:

Agency-wide PayBill: one PayBill number for the agency, with unique room account references across all its buildings.

Building-specific PayBills: different PayBill numbers for different buildings, with references unique within each relevant payment configuration.

I would support both in the data model, but start with agency-wide PayBill where the agency's payment provider setup permits it. It simplifies tenant instructions and centralizes reconciliation.
