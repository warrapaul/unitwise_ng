comprehensive UI/UX and product review
Your address feature is doing more than storing postal or delivery addresses. It is establishing a shared address foundation for three different workflows:
1. Property management: landlords create building addresses and associate their properties with Kenya's geographic hierarchy.
2. E-commerce delivery: customers save delivery addresses that buyers, riders and delivery teams can use.
3. Geospatial operations: Super Admin and E-commerce Admin maintain mapped regions, known landmarks and known buildings in QGIS/GIS so riders can locate destinations more reliably.


My main recommendation is to keep the common address hierarchy, introduce a clear distinction between a customer's address and a known delivery destination, and make verification and map coordinates first-class parts of the workflow.

The screenshots already show a good foundation: a progressive geographic registry, hierarchical selection, address defaults, map coordinates, known-building proximity, and rider-only delivery notes. The biggest production-readiness concerns are the relationship between these records, the consistency of the forms, and the meaning of verification.

Important architectural rule: a landlord-created building and an E-commerce Admin-created known delivery building are different business records, even if they happen to describe the same physical building. They can share geographic references and coordinates, but do not automatically merge their identities or permissions.

For example, a landlord may manage WAR1 Apartments in Unitwise. An E-commerce Admin may independently register a known building called WAR1 for deliveries. The customer can associate their address with the known delivery building without giving the e-commerce team access to the landlord's tenancy records.

This separation will help avoid unintended dependencies between property management and e-commerce.

7. How maintenance should connect to rent adjustments
Your existing rent modal already has a useful foundation for charges, waivers, adjustments, and payment recording. The missing piece is a controlled workflow linking an approved maintenance expense to a tenant's financial ledger.

The most important design rule is: a maintenance request must not automatically change rent or create a payable tenant charge.

A repair may be the landlord's responsibility, a tenant's responsibility, a shared cost, or a cost that cannot be charged to the tenant at all. That decision needs an authorized approval process based on the tenancy agreement, applicable law, and the agency's policies.
Recommended end-to-end workflow





Example using your existing rent modal
Suppose Room 101 has the following monthly statement:
Item	Amount (KES)
Rent	6,000
Garbage	200
Water	0
Total before maintenance	6,200

A plumbing repair costs KES 1,500.

The system should first record that as a maintenance expense and determine whether it is billable to the tenant. If an authorized person approves the tenant charge, the ledger can then display:
Item	Amount (KES)
Rent	6,000
Garbage	200
Water	0
Approved plumbing charge	1,500
Total due before payments	7,700

This is an illustration, not a recommendation that this particular repair should be charged to the tenant.

The maintenance charge should include a link back to the maintenance request, a reason, evidence or supporting documentation where applicable, the approver, the approval timestamp, and the applicable billing period.
Keep three financial concepts separate
- Property expense: The amount the landlord or agency incurs for repair work.
- Tenant charge: An amount that has been reviewed and authorized for billing to the tenant.
- Rent adjustment: A change to the actual rent obligation, such as an approved credit or rent reduction.
Do not use one generic adjustment field for all three.

I would also add the following to the existing rent modal:
- Source reference, e.g. MT-2026-0042.
- Charge category and explanation.
- Estimated versus actual cost.
- Approval status and approver.
- Reversal or credit functionality.
- Supporting receipt or invoice.
- Ledger history showing the original entry and all subsequent changes.
Prefer append-only financial transactions and reversing entries over silently editing an already posted charge. That makes reconciliation and audit reporting much more reliable.
8. WhatsApp integration: recommended approach
WhatsApp should be an additional communication channel connected to Unitwise conversations, not a replacement for the platform's internal messaging.

Use the official WhatsApp Business Platform rather than an unofficial WhatsApp Web automation library. Official documentation is available through Meta's WhatsApp documentation.
What the integration should support
Inbound WhatsApp messages

A tenant sends a WhatsApp message to the agency's registered business number. Unitwise identifies the sender where possible, finds the relevant conversation, and routes the message to the appropriate agency or support inbox.

If the tenant cannot be confidently matched, keep the message in an unassigned inbox rather than exposing another tenant's information.


Outbound WhatsApp messages

Authorized staff can respond from Unitwise, and the message is sent through the official API. Record the message, sender, recipient, channel, delivery state, and provider message ID.


Unified conversation history

Display internal and WhatsApp messages together when they belong to the same conversation. Label the source of each message and prevent duplicated webhook events from creating duplicate messages.


Notifications and automation

Send approved notifications for new messages, maintenance updates, scheduled visits, and selected announcements. Allow tenants to choose supported communication preferences.



A key architectural decision: internal messages versus WhatsApp
Keep a single canonical message record with a separate delivery record for each external channel.

For example, a staff member may send a maintenance update internally and also send it via WhatsApp to the tenant. The application should know whether those are two separate communications or the same message sent across multiple channels.

A practical data structure would include:
- conversation_id
- message_id
- sender_user_id
- channel (internal, whatsapp, and future channels)
- delivery_status
- sent_at, delivered_at, and read_at where available
- provider_error, if delivery failed
Do not assume that a message sent through WhatsApp has been delivered or read. Update the UI only when the provider's delivery-status webhook confirms the corresponding state.

For production, treat WhatsApp as a channel for individually addressed messages and approved business notifications unless the specific group capability you need is officially available and suitable for your account.

For example, a building-wide announcement can be represented as one Unitwise broadcast with multiple individual WhatsApp deliveries. The tenant should see the same announcement inside Unitwise and, if eligible and opted in, receive it on WhatsApp.
Important WhatsApp rules
- Obtain the necessary consent for WhatsApp communications.
- Support opt-out and communication preferences.
- Observe applicable template and customer-service messaging rules.
- Secure access tokens and webhook verification.
- Apply rate limits, retry policies, and idempotency.
- Store enough information to audit outbound messages and delivery failures.
- Avoid sending sensitive tenancy or financial details in a notification preview.
WhatsApp should be an integration behind the same authorization and messaging services as internal chat, not an independent application with a separate set of tenant permissions.

6. Rent, security deposits, and payments received

6.1 Separate agreed amounts from actual receipts
The application must distinguish at least four concepts:
Concept	Meaning	Example
Agreed rent	Contractual monthly rent	KES 6,000
Rent charge	Amount billed for a defined period	October rent: KES 6,000
Payment received	Actual money received	KES 6,000 via M-Pesa
Outstanding balance	Charges minus valid payments and adjustments	KES 0

Apply the same principle to deposits:
- Agreed deposit.
- Deposit amount due.
- Deposit actually received.
- Deposit held.
- Deposit refunded.
- Deposit applied to permitted charges.
- Outstanding deposit balance.
Do not treat an amount entered during tenant creation as proof of payment.

The user should explicitly confirm that money was received before the system creates a receipt or payment transaction. 
6.2 Improve the initial payment interface
For rent paid now, include:
- Amount received.
- Payment date and time, where necessary.
- Billing period covered.
- Payment method.
- Transaction or receipt reference.
- Payer and recipient, where relevant.
- Recording user.
- Notes or supporting evidence, where appropriate.
For a deposit, include the same essential information but link the receipt to the deposit ledger rather than to rent.

The current M-Pesa default is sensible for a Kenyan deployment, but the application should support configurable payment methods, such as bank transfer, cash, mobile money, and other locally supported methods.

For electronic payments, prefer reconciliation against provider-confirmed transactions rather than relying exclusively on manual references. (OR ADD A TODOLATER)
6.3 Add duplicate transaction protection
A payment reference may be entered twice, or a landlord may click Save twice because the interface appears unresponsive.

Implement idempotency and duplicate detection so repeated submissions do not create multiple receipts or apply the same payment more than once.

Where an integration supplies a unique transaction ID, enforce uniqueness in the appropriate financial scope. Where a manual payment lacks a unique provider ID, use a duplicate-warning workflow rather than assuming that two identical amounts must represent the same transaction.
6.4 Use immutable financial records
Once a payment has been posted, do not silently overwrite it.

If a landlord makes a mistake, the system should support a correction, reversal, or refund transaction with:
- The original transaction reference.
- The correction reason.
- The user making the correction.
- The timestamp.
- The resulting balance.
Financial records should be auditable and linked to the appropriate tenancy and accounting period.
6.5 Deposits need a lifecycle of their own
A security deposit is not simply another payment. It may be held for a long time, refunded at the end of the tenancy, or subject to permitted deductions.

- Amount agreed and received.
- Date received.
- Payment method and reference.
- Current deposit balance.
- Any deductions and their documented reasons.
- Refund amount and date.
- Refund transaction reference.
- Outstanding deposit balance, where applicable.
Deposit handling and permissible deductions must follow the applicable tenancy and financial rules for the deployment jurisdiction.

4. Important rules before you automate matching
I recommend automating matching and reconciliation assistance first, not treating two pasted messages as conclusive proof that money was received.

A pasted SMS can be edited or fabricated. Even if two messages contain the same code and amount, they might originate from copied or manipulated text. A real M-Pesa or bank integration can provide stronger verification.

Check	Recommended behaviour
Transaction reference	Exact normalized match, including case normalization where appropriate.
Amount	Exact match to the transaction amount, not just the outstanding bill balance.

UnitWise action

Valid transaction, unique reference, room identified, no conflicting claim

Valid transaction and room identified, but no tenant claim exists

I would support both in the data model, but start with agency-wide PayBill where the agency's payment provider setup permits it. It simplifies tenant instructions and centralizes reconciliation.

the following recommendations
the dashboard has a solid foundation for an agency administrator, but it needs improvements to its data presentation, operational priorities, and visual hierarchy before it is production-ready.

Compared with the tenant dashboard, this dashboard has a different purpose. A tenant needs to understand their own tenancy and payments. An AGENCY_ADMIN needs to understand the performance of the entire property portfolio, identify problems, and take action across multiple buildings, tenants, leases, and payments.

The current UI already has the right building blocks: rental collection, occupancy, tenant counts, lease status, building-level billing, arrears, and operational navigation. I would preserve those features while making the dashboard more actionable and fixing some important inconsistencies in the displayed figures.
1. What is already working well

Rental collection summary

Showing expected rent, collected rent, outstanding balances, and collection percentage gives the agency administrator a useful overview of financial performance.




Building-level operational table

The building table provides a practical way to compare room availability, occupancy, tenant numbers, arrears, billing status, and repairs across the portfolio.




Useful management metrics

Occupancy, tenants, leases, portfolio size, and move-ins and move-outs are relevant to an agency administrator's daily work.




A task-oriented sidebar

The navigation exposes important operational areas, including payments to confirm, overdue accounts, deposits, meter readings, rent adjustments, leases, and applications. These are valuable features for a property management platform.



The foundation is appropriate. The biggest opportunity is to turn the dashboard from a collection of statistics into a portfolio management and decision-making workspace.
2. Critical issues to address first

1. Fix metric formatting and data consistency
Critical
The building table appears to merge values such as 1 8% under Occupied and 6,00077% under Collected. These should be separate, clearly aligned values. The 77% collection rate itself is correct for KES 6,000 collected against KES 7,801 expected.

More importantly, the dashboard shows one active tenant, zero active leases, and one lease awaiting signature, while the building has one occupied room. This could be a legitimate workflow state, but the dashboard should make that relationship understandable rather than appearing contradictory.

Recommendation: Check the underlying metric definitions, table column sizing, and the relationship between occupancy, tenant status, and lease status.




2. Make “1 thing needs attention” useful

The alert bar is a good concept, but it does not identify the problem until the administrator expands it.

Recommendation: Show a short description, severity, and direct action. For example, “1 lease awaiting signature” with a Review lease action, if that is the outstanding task. If multiple types of issues exist, group them by urgency.




3. Reduce the oversized header

The header occupies a large portion of the initial viewport but contains little information. The large blank space between the navigation shortcuts and the rental collection card is not earning its space.

Recommendation: Make the header more compact. Place the page title, reporting period, relevant filters, and a small set of primary actions in a single, well-organized area.




4. Strengthen the financial overview

The collection card is useful, but the administrator cannot immediately tell whether 77% collection is good or poor for the current point in the month.

Recommendation: Add context such as the previous month's collection rate, change from the previous period, and the number of accounts overdue. Make sure comparisons use equivalent billing periods and clearly distinguish overdue arrears from amounts that are not yet due.




5. Make metrics actionable

The cards show counts, but the administrator needs to know which records make up those counts.

Recommendation: Let administrators click “Vacant,” “Awaiting signature,” “Outstanding,” and “Move-ins” to open filtered lists. Every important metric should lead to the relevant operational workflow.



3. Review of each dashboard section
A. Header and quick navigation
The current header says “Default overview” and shows an AGENCY_ADMIN badge. It is functional, but not particularly informative or polished.

Recommended changes:
- Replace “Default overview” with Agency overview or Portfolio overview.
- Show the agency name and reporting period where useful.
- Keep the AGENCY_ADMIN role as account metadata rather than a prominent page element.
- Replace the generic shortcut chips with meaningful primary actions, such as Add building, Add tenant, or Review payments, subject to permissions.
- Remove duplicate navigation links where the sidebar already provides the same destinations.
The shortcuts for Buildings, Tenants, Rent payments, and Arrears are useful, but should either be clearly presented as quick actions or omitted in favor of a more purposeful summary.

- Distinguish draft leases from leases awaiting the landlord's signature and leases awaiting the tenant's signature.
- Provide a clear lease lifecycle, from draft through signature to active tenancy.
- Ensure the occupancy and tenant summaries explain situations where a tenant occupies a room but the lease is not yet active.
A lease dashboard is most useful when it tells the administrator what needs attention, not just how many leases exist.
G. Portfolio

These are useful headline metrics. For an agency with multiple properties, I would add links to the building list and show a small amount of trend information where it helps decision-making.

For example, the administrator could compare the current room count with the previous period or see whether occupancy is improving. Avoid adding trend indicators unless historical data is available and the comparison is meaningful.
H. Moves in and out
This card shows one move-in, zero move-outs, zero transfers, and a net change of one.

The concept is useful, but the dashboard needs a clear period for these metrics. Otherwise, an administrator cannot tell whether the counts refer to today, this month, or the lifetime of the agency.

Recommendation: Add a reporting-period label and make each metric open a list of relevant tenancy events. The net change should be clearly defined as move-ins minus move-outs, with transfers treated according to the system's business rules.

4. What is missing from a production-ready landlord dashboard?
The current dashboard focuses mainly on rent collection and tenancy statistics. For an agency administrator managing multiple buildings, I would also make maintenance, operational tasks, and financial follow-up first-class parts of the experience.

1. Actionable tasks and approvals

Surface leases awaiting signature, tenant applications requiring review, payments awaiting confirmation, outstanding meter readings, and bills requiring approval. Every task should have an owner, status, and direct action where appropriate.




2. Maintenance and repairs overview

Show open repairs, urgent requests, overdue maintenance tasks, and unresolved issues by building. A count of zero is useful only if the system confirms that the count is current and includes all relevant requests.




3. Financial performance and reconciliation

In addition to rent collection, consider showing unallocated payments, payment reports awaiting confirmation, deposit liabilities, adjustments, and a link to financial reports. Keep actual cash received distinct from amounts reported by tenants but not yet confirmed.




4. Upcoming events

Highlight lease expirations, scheduled inspections, expected move-ins, and upcoming rent billing dates. Prioritize items that require action rather than filling the dashboard with a general calendar.




5. Portfolio-wide property context

As the agency grows, a compact property comparison or location view could help administrators identify low occupancy, high arrears, and maintenance hotspots. This can be introduced later, rather than adding a map simply for decoration.



I would not add all five as large dashboard cards. The dashboard should summarize them, with detailed workflows remaining in their dedicated sections.
5. Visual design and layout improvements
The existing visual style is consistent with the tenant dashboard: muted green accents, neutral backgrounds, rounded cards, and a left-hand navigation. I would preserve that design system.

The most noticeable issue is the use of space. The header is tall, the collection summary floats on the right, and the building table stretches across the page. The lower cards have a reasonable grid, but some contain only a few values and large amounts of unused space.









Compact the header

Reduce its vertical height, put the reporting period and key actions in a consistent toolbar, and use the recovered space for financial KPIs and actionable alerts.












Improve data density and alignment

Give numeric columns adequate width, right-align monetary amounts, prevent adjacent values from running together, and support horizontal scrolling or a responsive alternative on narrow screens.












Standardize KPI cards

Use consistent heading sizes, label placement, padding, and status treatments. Avoid giving every statistic equal emphasis; overdue balances and pending approvals deserve more attention than total portfolio size.












Test responsive layouts

At narrower widths, stack the KPI cards, allow the table to adapt, collapse the sidebar into a drawer, and keep the most important actions available without excessive scrolling.



I would also check keyboard navigation, focus indicators, text contrast, accessible labels, loading states, and empty states. A production dashboard should communicate when data is loading, when there is no data, and when data cannot be retrieved, rather than displaying misleading zeros.
